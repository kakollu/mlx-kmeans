# Six Apple Silicon machines — September 24, 2026

Until now every number in this repository came from one machine. These were measured on six, in an Apple
Store on the afternoon of September 24, using `./no_admin_bench.sh` on display units with no administrator
access. Each run printed SHA-256 fingerprints of the files it executed, and all six match this repository's
`mlx_kmeans/core.py` (`1c45d961…85e6`) and `quick_bench.py` (`1e8953ab…7463`) at the revision current that
day, so every machine ran identical code.

All times are seconds per k-means pass: one assignment plus one centre update over every row, median of
timed runs after a warm-up. Lower is better.

## What this establishes

**A 16 GB MacBook Air clusters 37 million rows by 32 features into 1,024 clusters**, at 1.9 s per pass, and
handles ten million rows by twelve features in 26.5 ms. The laptop is not the obstacle people expect it to be.

**Even a phone-class chip is useful.** The A18 Pro MacBook, with five GPU cores and 8 GB, reached 16.7M rows
and did 10M × 12 in 54 ms — more data than most analyses involve.

**The spread across the range is smaller than the hardware suggests.** From five GPU cores to forty is an
eightfold difference in the specification; on 10M × 12 it is 7.6x (0.0542 to 0.0071), and on the small
1M × 8 case only 3.1x. Low-dimensional passes are limited by memory, not by how many cores are available to
wait on it.

## Limits of these measurements

These runs were made on in-store display machines without asking a staff member first. Nothing persisted -
everything went to `/tmp`, which is cleared on restart, and no account or data was touched - but permission
should have been sought, and the README now says so for anyone repeating this on a machine they do not own.

These are **retail display units**, not controlled conditions: unknown thermal state, display software
running, no power or background-load control, and one run each rather than a distribution. They establish
that the library runs and roughly how fast; they are not a careful cross-machine study.

Two specific cautions:

**Small shapes are noisy.** The 20-core M5 Pro MacBook Pro beat the 32-core M5 Max Studio on the two
smallest cases (0.0025 against 0.0045 s). A 1M × 8 pass takes a few milliseconds, which is short enough for
background activity to dominate. The large cases are the trustworthy ones.

**The bandwidth column is not comparable across rows.** `quick_bench.py` reads a 1.0 GB buffer when the GPU
budget is above 16 GB and a 0.2 GB buffer otherwise, and the smaller buffer under-reports. That is why the
M6 mini appears slower than the M5 Air (56 against 71 GB/s) despite being a later chip: they are the same
test at the same buffer size, but neither is the machine's real peak. Treat the four low-memory rows as a
floor.

No comparison against scikit-learn was possible: `no_admin_bench.sh` installs only `mlx` and `numpy`, so
these are absolute times rather than speedups.
